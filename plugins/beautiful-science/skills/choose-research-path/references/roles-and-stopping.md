# Investigator, supervisor, and stopping

## Investigator

Translate the promising feature into a claim with a boundary. Expose the hardest unsupported implication, not only the easiest favorable demonstration. Propose a useful competing explanation and a check that could make the preferred path lose. Return the result, evidence status, costs, and the precise open obligation.

Early exploration can be justified by a tractable question: find a useful representation, derive a nontrivial necessary condition, characterize a failure, or find a counterexample. Do not pretend that an immature idea already has a decisive experiment. State the prerequisite work that could make it testable.

## Supervisor

Begin from the scientific goal and inspect the evidence-bearing artifact when available: theorem statement, certificate, assumptions, dataset/design, raw residuals, resource accounting, or actual run log. An investigator's confidence is not a substitute. If raw evidence is unavailable, label the judgment provisional and say which verification is missing.

Compare the proposed next step with a credible alternative for the same goal. Ask what changed since the last review and which possibility the next work can distinguish. Preserve disagreements and the evidence that would resolve them; choose conditional recommendations when the missing information genuinely matters.

Separate generating an idea from checking it. Another agent or implementation is not automatically independent: both may share the wrong specification, algorithm, data leakage, instrument, or prior answer. Give a checker the exact claim and raw artifacts, not the desired verdict. No extra agent is required for small work; this separation can be a second explicit pass.

A supervisor's decision is about work allocation and claim status. It is not permission to cancel someone else's running job or delete their work. Respect the user's task, persistence instructions, and authorization boundaries.

## Four decisions

| Decision | When it fits | What to name |
|---|---|---|
| Continue | A live path has concrete progress and a relevant feasible next obligation | The incremental result and reassessment event |
| Bounded test | A specific uncertainty can change the choice | The discriminator, validity conditions, outcome branches, and evidence-derived effort |
| Change method | The target remains valuable but this method has a diagnosed obstruction or dominated cost | The obstruction, surviving assets, and a materially different mechanism |
| Park this claim/path | The claim is refuted in scope, currently non-identifiable, infeasible for the objective, or lacks an informative next step | Exact scope being parked and a concrete condition for reopening |

“Park” may be reversible. “This model's prediction is contradicted” is stronger and different. A failed application can leave a mathematical structure, measurement method, restricted theorem, or small-instance algorithm worth keeping under its corrected description.

## Derive budgets and stop conditions from the question

1. Specify the distinction needed for the decision: a proof obligation, target effect, required accuracy, scaling bound, or identifiable parameter range
2. Determine what evidence could resolve it and whether the design is capable of doing so
3. Estimate resources from that evidence: case count and certificate cost, complexity bound, pilot variance and detectable effect, simulation of the proposed measurement, or the user's actual resource constraint
4. State observable outcomes and their implications before spending the next allocation

For statistics, use an appropriate prespecified fixed-sample or sequential design; account for dependence, repeated looks, multiplicity, and practical effect size. If variance or feasible effect separation is unknown, estimate it first. Do not fabricate a universal run count, timebox, Bayes factor, or threshold.

For proofs, use a finite obligation or a structural milestone rather than a made-up number of failed attempts. A proof problem can remain difficult and fruitful for a long time. If no resource estimate is possible, say so and offer the smallest useful formalization or feasibility check rather than disguising ignorance as a budget.

External limits are real: a deadline, safety limit, funding cap, or available hardware can bound work. State that they are constraints on activity, not evidence against the theorem or physical hypothesis. If the user asks for a concrete plan and a necessary budget choice remains theirs, ask the one decision needed.

## Repetition versus progress

Before another run, identify what differs: hypothesis, implementation, measurement validity, sampling uncertainty, input regime, or a new implication. More samples can be useful when they resolve sampling uncertainty under a valid design. They cannot fix structural non-identifiability, circular definitions, a false lemma, hidden exponential storage, or an unchanged effect far below the target.

A plateau is diagnostic, not automatically fatal. Reaching the same conclusion after genuinely independent tests can increase reliability. Repeating the same test under the same shared defect does not.

Practical review signals:
- Testing is repeatedly deferred → identify the prerequisite and why it is necessary
- Each failure introduces an independent rescue assumption → evaluate the revised model and its new prediction
- Benchmark numbers change without a changed hypothesis → inspect variance, selection, and full pipeline
- Long work produces checked lemmas, constraints, or tools → recognize progress, even before the final breakthrough
- A simple model works only after deleting hard cases → restore the target scope or state the narrower claim

Record failed predictions and abandoned branches alongside successes. A compact dated note of the initial expectation and actual outcome prevents hindsight from converting every success into evidence that taste was always right.
